# Title, Abstract and Introduction (draft)

**Title:** Do enforcement actions lower fine particulate pollution in Dhaka? A pre-registered event study, 2016–2025

**Author:** Prantique Roy, Dhaka, Bangladesh

## Abstract
Dhaka has some of the highest fine particulate matter (PM2.5) concentrations of any major city, with annual levels five to six times the national standard. Since 2019, the High Court has issued repeated directives on air pollution, and the Department of Environment has run mobile courts and brick kiln demolition drives, but it is not known whether these actions improved air quality. This study used nine years of hourly PM2.5 data from the U.S. Embassy monitor in Dhaka (2016–2025) and a machine learning model of weather to calculate weather-adjusted PM2.5, and compared levels in the 30 days before and after 12 verified enforcement actions, grouped into 10 clusters. The analysis was pre-registered. The method detected known falls in activity: weather-adjusted PM2.5 fell by 43.5% during Eid holidays and 19.7% during the COVID-19 general holiday. In contrast, weather-adjusted PM2.5 was on average 7.3% higher after enforcement actions than before (p = 0.97 for the hypothesis of a fall), and this result held in all robustness checks. Exploratory checks suggested that the rise reflected a return to normal levels after slightly cleaner months, not a worsening caused by the actions. Enforcement actions therefore left no detectable trace in air quality. Pollution from outside Bangladesh, a focus on brick kilns over other sources, and weak implementation of court orders may explain this result.

## Introduction
Dhaka, the capital of Bangladesh and home to more than 20 million people, has some of the most polluted air of any large city. At the U.S. Embassy monitor, mean PM2.5 concentrations reach about 165–175 µg/m³ in winter and fall to about 30–35 µg/m³ during the monsoon; annual means of about 80–98 µg/m³ are five to six times the Bangladesh annual standard of 15 µg/m³, and the daily standard of 65 µg/m³ is exceeded on 144 to 188 days a year (Sarwar et al., 2023). Long-term exposure to PM2.5 is linked to heart and lung disease and early death.

Over the past several years, the government and the courts have taken repeated action against this problem. In January 2019, the rights organisation Human Rights and Peace for Bangladesh filed a petition asking the High Court to act on air pollution in Dhaka. The court responded with a series of orders, including a nine-point directive in January 2020 that required authorities to cover trucks and construction sites, spray water on roads, seize vehicles emitting black smoke and shut illegal brick kilns around the city. It issued further orders and summonses in 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023 and 2025. Alongside the court case, the Department of Environment (DoE) has carried out mobile court drives and demolished illegal brick kilns.

Whether these actions improved the air has not been tested. Earlier studies have described the seasonal and daily patterns of PM2.5 in Dhaka (Sarwar et al., 2023) or measured the effect of sudden falls in activity. Salam et al. (2021) found that PM2.5 at Dhaka University was about 54 µg/m³ on politically motivated lockdown days, compared with about 160 µg/m³ on normal days. Sarkar and Khan (2021) reported an 11% fall during the COVID-19 lockdown, but did not account for weather, and the fall was not statistically clear. Begum and Hopke (2018) found that some earlier policies, such as the 2002 ban on two-stroke engines, did not clearly reduce the contribution of the targeted source. However, no study has systematically tested whether enforcement actions in Dhaka are followed by cleaner air.

Testing this is difficult because weather has a much larger effect on PM2.5 than most policies are likely to have. Rain, wind and the height of the air layer near the ground can change PM2.5 by a large amount from one week to the next, and pollution from outside the country can make up about half of the city's PM2.5 (Sarwar et al., 2023). A simple comparison of pollution before and after an order could therefore reflect a change in the weather rather than an effect of the order.

This study asked whether weather-adjusted PM2.5 in Dhaka is lower in the 30 days after an enforcement action than in the 30 days before. A machine learning model was used to remove the effect of weather from nine years of hourly data, and the method was first tested on Eid holidays and the COVID-19 general holiday, when activity is known to have fallen. Enforcement actions were taken from a timeline built from news reports, in which each date was confirmed by two independent sources. The main hypothesis (H1) was that weather-adjusted PM2.5 falls after enforcement actions. The analysis plan was registered on the Open Science Framework before the main test was run.
