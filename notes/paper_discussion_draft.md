# Discussion (draft)

This study tested whether air quality in Dhaka improved after court orders and enforcement drives against air pollution between 2019 and 2025. It found no evidence that it did. In the month after an enforcement action, weather-adjusted PM2.5 was on average 7.3% higher than in the month before, and the result held in every pre-registered robustness check. This is not because the method was unable to detect change. The same approach found clear falls during Eid holidays (−43.5%) and the COVID-19 general holiday (−19.7%). If enforcement actions had reduced pollution by a similar amount, the study would have detected it. Exploratory checks suggest that the rise does not mean the actions made the air worse: actions tended to follow months that were cleaner than usual, and pollution afterwards returned to its normal level.

The most likely reason for the absence of an effect is that a large part of Dhaka's pollution is outside the reach of local enforcement. Sarwar et al. (2023) estimated that about half of the city's annual PM2.5, and about 43% in the dry season, comes from outside Bangladesh. Even a fully enforced local crackdown could therefore change only part of what the monitor measures. A second reason is that enforcement focused mainly on brick kilns. Of the 12 actions tested, six concerned kilns, while vehicles, road dust, construction and small industries received less sustained attention. Air quality researchers have made the same criticism of earlier government projects, which targeted kilns but did not address other sources together (The Business Standard, 2022).

Pollution from outside the country cannot, however, be the whole explanation. During Eid, when local traffic, construction and industry slow down, weather-adjusted PM2.5 fell by more than 40%, which shows that local sources matter a great deal. The second part of the explanation is that orders were often not carried out. The High Court itself repeatedly said that its directives were being ignored: in January 2023 the bench said it was "ashamed" to have to repeat its orders, and in February 2023 it found that none had been meaningfully implemented. In 2022, the Department of Environment (DoE) reported that 314 of 411 illegal kilns around Dhaka had been shut, but about 275 legal challenges and six-month stay orders had delayed action against others, and four district administrations reported closing 114 kilns while 318 were said to be still operating. In 2019, the DoE had only one magistrate in Dhaka to run mobile courts, and in 2022 Transparency International Bangladesh reported that about 60% of DoE posts were vacant. An order on paper does not change what kilns, trucks and construction sites do. If enforcement was weak or delayed, the date of a court order would not mark a real change in emissions, and no effect would be expected.

These results are consistent with the view of the petitioners in the High Court case, who argued that pollution fell when directives were implemented and rose again when enforcement became lax (The Daily Observer, 2025). They also add to earlier work on the COVID-19 lockdown. Sarkar and Khan (2021) reported an 11% fall in PM2.5 during the lockdown without accounting for weather, and their result was not statistically clear. After removing the effect of weather, this study found a larger fall of about 20%, which suggests that weather can hide real changes in emissions if it is not taken into account.

The study has several limitations. It relies on a single monitor, at the U.S. Embassy in Baridhara, which may not reflect changes elsewhere in the city or near kiln areas. Only 10 clusters of actions could be tested, so effects smaller than about 13% could not be detected. The official date of an order may not match when enforcement actually began, and neighbouring actions were 44 to 53 days apart, so their before and after periods partly overlap. Only two of the verified actions were physical enforcement drives, so the study could not test whether drives work better than court orders (H3). Finally, the minimum data rule for shorter windows was changed from the pre-registered plan, although this did not affect the main test.

For enforcement to have a measurable effect, four changes appear necessary. Enforcement would need to continue for months rather than consist of a single order or drive. Records of what was enforced, where and when should be made public, so that compliance can be checked independently. The DoE would need more staff and magistrates. And because a large share of Dhaka's pollution comes from across the border, local action would need to be combined with regional cooperation, particularly with India.

Future work could test physical enforcement directly by building a larger list of demolition and mobile court drives from Bangla-language newspapers, and could use low-cost sensors near kiln areas, such as Savar, which was declared a degraded air shed in August 2025, to measure local effects that a single city-centre monitor may miss.
